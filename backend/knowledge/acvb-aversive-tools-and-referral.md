The ACVB holds that electronic collars carry significant risk of fear, aggression,
physical pain, and long-term welfare harm, and that they perform no better than
reward-based methods. Reward-based training is at least as effective for recall,
reactivity, and behavior modification.

Pairing a warning sound with a shock is fear conditioning. The anxiety it creates can
attach to the warning cue, to the handler, to the training place, or to anything else
that happened to be present, and it does not teach the dog an alternative behavior.

On the cases where the stakes are highest, the ACVB is explicit: when a behavior
problem is serious enough that euthanasia or rehoming is on the table, or when there
is risk of human injury or of the dog hurting itself, the standard of care is
assessment by a veterinarian with advanced behavior expertise, ideally a board-certified
veterinary behaviorist (DACVB). Board-certified behaviorists assess risk, look for
medical causes, build a treatment plan, and prescribe behavioral medication when it is
needed.

There is no evidence that aversive tools reduce euthanasia risk. Multiple studies
associate them with more aggression, more fear, and lower treatment success.

The ACVB encourages early referral, not referral as a last resort, because outcomes are
better and risk is lower when a specialist is involved sooner.
