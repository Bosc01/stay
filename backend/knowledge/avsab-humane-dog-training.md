Reward-based methods are the recommended approach for all dog training and for
the treatment of behavior problems. There is no evidence that aversive training is
necessary for either.

Methods that rely on force, pain, or emotional or physical discomfort should not be
used. Specific tools and techniques the statement says to avoid: choke chains, prong
collars, electronic shock collars, squirt bottles, shaker cans, compressed air, shouting,
staring, alpha rolls, dominance downs, leash jerking, physical force, and flooding
(forced exposure). The dog must always be able to opt out of a training session.

Training works best when it teaches the dog what to do rather than punishing what
it already does. Common issues such as jumping, barking, and house soiling can be
handled by arranging the environment so the unwanted behavior is hard to perform,
then reinforcing the response you want instead.

More serious concerns, specifically aggression, anxiety, and fear, need a plan with
three parts: environmental management, behavior modification, and in some cases
medication. Management includes avoiding the situations that set the behavior off
and keeping everyone safe while the plan runs.

Documented fallout from aversive methods: increased anxiety, fear-related aggression,
avoidance, learned helplessness, stress-related behaviors during training, and a worse
relationship with the owner. Dogs trained with rewards show higher obedience than
dogs trained with aversives or with a mix of both.

Aggression is not an exception to this standard. The statement says animals with
aggression should be treated with humane methods, with no exceptions, and that a
trainer who is stuck should refer to a veterinarian, a board-certified veterinary
behaviorist, or a certified applied animal behaviorist.
