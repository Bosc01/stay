Separation anxiety is distress at being separated from the person the dog is attached
to. Escape attempts are often extreme and can injure the dog, and destruction tends to
cluster around exit points, doors and windows.

Rule other things out first. Incomplete house training, submissive or excitement
urination, urine marking, plain boredom, and medical causes such as incontinence, a
urinary tract infection, or a medication that increases urination all look similar.
True separation anxiety shows distress signs, including drooling and anxiety during the
owner's departure routine, and those signs appear only when the dog is alone.

For mild cases: counterconditioning. Every departure, give a puzzle toy stuffed with
food that takes at least 20 to 30 minutes to finish, for example a frozen stuffed
rubber toy. Take the toy away as soon as you get home so it only exists during
departures.

For moderate to severe cases: graduated departures, and they have to stay under the
dog's threshold. Start with out-of-sight stays at an interior door for one to two
seconds. Increase by seconds per session, not minutes. Only increase when the dog shows
no stress signs such as panting, pacing, or dilated pupils. The dog has to tolerate
40 minutes before you jump to larger increments, then build to 90 minutes, then to
four-to-eight-hour absences. Several short sessions a day moves faster than one.

Crating helps only if the dog already treats the crate as a safe place. If the dog shows
distress in the crate, use a baby gate and a single room instead.

Do not scold or punish. The page states these behaviors are not disobedience or spite.
Getting a second dog is not recommended as a fix. Consult a veterinarian, a
board-certified veterinary behaviorist (DACVB), or a certified applied animal
behaviorist (CAAB or ACAAB) before medication or a complex protocol.
