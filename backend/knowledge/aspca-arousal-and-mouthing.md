Playful mouthing and aggressive mouthing look different. In play the body and face are
relaxed and there is little tension. In aggression the body is stiff, the teeth show,
and the bite is faster and hurts more.

Bite inhibition: allow mouthing during play, then yelp when the dog bites hard, let the
hand go limp, pause briefly, and resume. Lower the threshold for what counts as too hard
over time.

Redirection: put a toy or chew in the dog's mouth instead of a hand or foot, and keep
tug toys within reach so the substitution is always available.

Time-outs: withdraw attention for 30 to 60 seconds after teeth touch skin, either by
leaving the room or by calmly using a leash to settle the dog.

Outlets and impulse control: frequent play with other healthy vaccinated dogs, plenty of
toys, and cues that build self-control such as sit, wait, and leave it.

Do not slap, hit, or scruff-shake, and do not use physical punishment. These escalate
aggression. Avoid jerky hand movements, finger-waving games, and snatching the hand
away, all of which invite more biting.

If aggression rather than play is suspected, consult a certified applied animal
behaviorist, a board-certified veterinary behaviorist, or a certified professional dog
trainer qualified in aggression.
