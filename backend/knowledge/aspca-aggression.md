Aggression is the most common and most serious behavior problem in dogs, and the top
reason owners seek professional help.

Dogs escalate through a fairly predictable sequence: going rigid, guttural barking,
lunging with no contact, mouthing, muzzle punching, growling, showing teeth, snarling,
snapping, nipping, biting with increasing pressure, then repeated bites. Dogs rarely
bite with no warning at all, though the gap between warning and contact can be
milliseconds.

The page classifies aggression by function, including territorial, protective,
possessive, fear-based, defensive, social or status-related, frustration-elicited,
redirected, pain-elicited, sex-related, and predatory.

Four things make the outlook worse: a previous bite, higher severity, meaning actual
bites rather than warnings, low predictability, meaning little warning before a bite,
and a child as the target. Aggression toward children is described as exceedingly
difficult to treat because of the safety problem, with a slim chance the dog can be
trusted around them.

Punishment-based approaches, specifically alpha rolls, scruff shakes, and hanging,
typically make aggression worse.

See a veterinarian first, before anything else, to rule out a medical cause. Then work
with a certified applied animal behaviorist (CAAB or ACAAB), a board-certified
veterinary behaviorist (DACVB), or a certified professional dog trainer (CPDT) with
specific expertise in aggression.
