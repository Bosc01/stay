Resource guarding runs from mild, running off with an item or growling when someone
approaches, to severe, biting or chasing a person away. Some dogs guard only one class
of item, others guard everything.

The recommended program is desensitization and counterconditioning in seven stages.
Cut high-value food the dog does not normally get, chicken, beef, hot dog, or cheese,
into bite-sized pieces.

Stage one: stand several feet away while the dog eats its normal meal, say something
conversational such as "what have you got there," and toss a special treat toward the
bowl every few seconds. Ten consecutive relaxed meals before moving on.

Stages two through six close the distance and add interaction a step at a time, moving
nearer, bending down, touching the bowl, picking the bowl up, always paying with food
better than what is in the bowl. Each stage needs ten consecutive relaxed meals before
the next. Stage seven repeats the whole sequence with each family member individually.

Do not punish, intimidate, or try to dominate the dog. Do not take the food away and do
not hover over the bowl. The page states this is dangerous, unnecessary, and can make
guarding worse.

Stop the exercises and get a professional if the dog is stressed enough to stop eating
but still guards, if it shows any aggression signs, stiffening, gulping the food,
freezing, growling, hard staring, snapping, snarling, biting, or chasing, if progress
stalls, if anyone under 18 lives in the home, or if the owner is unsure about safety.
The page is explicit that an owner who thinks the dog is likely to bite should not
attempt this alone. Contact a certified applied animal behaviorist (CAAB or ACAAB), a
board-certified veterinary behaviorist (DACVB), or a certified professional dog trainer
(CPDT) qualified in this area.
